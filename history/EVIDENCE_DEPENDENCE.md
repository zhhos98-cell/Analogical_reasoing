# Evidence Dependence — When Several Historical Analogies Are Really One Piece of Evidence

**Snapshot: 21 August 2026.**

Multi-precedent reasoning looks stronger than single-case analogy because repeated structural roles across several historical events can distinguish mechanism from coincidence. But this gain depends on a difficult condition:

> **the apparent confirmations must contribute sufficiently independent evidence.**

This is not merely a philosophical caution. It is an explicit mathematical assumption in the most advanced current historical-analogy system, CANA.

---

## 1. CANA's cross-analogy result assumes conditional independence

**Chen et al., _Analogical Deep Research_, 2026.**  
Canonical: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13602

For structural position `s`, CANA defines a binary signal that analogy `k` confirms that position. Its Cross-Analogy Confirmation theorem assumes the confirmation signals are **conditionally independent across analogies given whether the target position is genuinely active**.

Under that assumption, each independent confirming analogy multiplies posterior odds. In the calibrated example in the paper, two independent analogies are enough to exceed the chosen confidence threshold for a structural position.

This formalizes an intuitive historical practice:

`the same role appearing independently in several different precedents`

is much stronger than

`one vivid precedent containing that role`.

---

## 2. CANA already filters one important class of false independence

The implementation is more careful than a simple `count analogies` rule.

Its self-analogy filter distinguishes:

- `IDENTITY` — same event / alias / part-whole relation;
- `SIBLING_TRIVIAL` — distinct events whose similarity is explained by a shared institutional series, common actor/campaign, or other common cause;
- `DISTINCT` — genuinely different events to be evaluated on structural merits.

The prompt explicitly says that sibling events may be **independent events yet still not provide useful analogical evidence** because their resemblance is generated by a common institution/cause rather than by a reusable structural mechanism.

Examples include:

- installments of the same diplomatic/political series;
- recurring institutional meetings or ceremonies;
- the same actor performing the same action in the same era;
- sub-events and phases of one larger event.

This is a meaningful first dependence-control layer.

---

# 3. The unresolved dependence problem is deeper

`SIBLING_TRIVIAL` catches obvious lineage. Historical evidence can be dependent in subtler ways.

## D1 — institutional genealogy

Two events may occur in different countries and decades but inherit the same institutional template through direct borrowing.

Their shared mechanism is real, but they are not two independent discoveries of that mechanism.

## D2 — policy diffusion / imitation

A later actor may explicitly copy an earlier policy or strategic playbook. Counting both as independent precedents double-counts one transmission chain.

## D3 — technological lineage

Two deployment histories may share the same underlying infrastructure, supply chain or predecessor technology. They can look like separate historical technologies while reflecting one developmental pathway.

## D4 — common exogenous shock

Several episodes can confirm the same apparent mechanism because all were driven by one global shock: oil price, monetary regime, pandemic, war, regulatory wave.

## D5 — dataset / source dependence

The underlying descriptions of several “different” historical cases may be derived from the same reference work, dataset schema, encyclopedia narrative or event-coding ontology.

This is particularly relevant to LLM systems because training data can silently reproduce the same canonical account across many documents.

## D6 — historiographic dependence

Later interpretations can consciously model one historical case on another. If the LLM's representations of both events inherit that historiographical comparison, cross-analogy confirmation may partly measure **narrative repetition** rather than independent historical evidence.

## D7 — selection dependence

The retrieval algorithm can create correlated evidence by repeatedly searching around the same mechanism hypothesis. Several returned sources may be “independent” real-world events but conditionally dependent given the retrieval path that selected them.

CANA's theorem notes that its probabilities are understood conditional on the selection rule; operationally estimating this selection dependence remains difficult.

---

# 4. Human structured-analogy evidence gives a useful but incomplete baseline

**Green & Armstrong, _Structured analogies for forecasting_ (2007).**

Experts who generated multiple analogies generally produced better forecasts than those relying on a single analogy; forecasts based on plural analogies were substantially more accurate in their experiment.

The result supports **precedent pluralism**, but it does not solve statistical/historical dependence. Two analogies can be plural yet derive from one common historical process.

Modern systems should therefore distinguish:

`analogue count`

from

`effective independent precedent count`.

---

# 5. A candidate dependence graph

A future historical-analogy controller could construct a graph over retrieved precedents.

Nodes:

`historical event / episode / source precedent`

Edges:

- direct institutional lineage;
- actor imitation;
- technological ancestry;
- common exogenous shock;
- shared source/dataset provenance;
- shared historiographic genealogy;
- nested/part-whole relationship;
- retrieval-cluster similarity.

Then confirmation should be discounted by dependence rather than counted naively.

A simple conceptual quantity is:

`effective_support(position s) < raw_number_of_confirming_analogies`

whenever several sources occupy one dependence cluster.

The exact estimator could draw on hierarchical Bayesian models, clustered evidence aggregation, causal graphs or effective-sample-size ideas. The important requirement is representational: the system must first know **why two precedents are related to each other**, not only why each resembles the target.

---

# 6. Dependence-aware cross-analogy output

Instead of:

```text
Three precedents confirm mechanism M.
```

A mature output should look closer to:

```text
Position: liquidity-amplifier

Confirming precedents:
  S1: 1907 Panic
  S2: LTCM 1998
  S3: crisis X

Dependence assessment:
  S1–S2: low known historical dependence
  S2–S3: medium; shared regulatory/market architecture
  S1–S3: low

Raw confirmations: 3
Effective independent support: approximately 2.x / qualitative MEDIUM-HIGH

Remaining concern:
  S2 and S3 may reflect the same institutional mechanism lineage.
```

The exact numerical score is secondary. The epistemic advance is preventing `three stories` from automatically becoming `three independent observations`.

---

# 7. Why this matters for the branch

Historical analogy is especially vulnerable to false confirmation because history is **path dependent**. Cases are connected by:

- imitation;
- institutions;
- memory;
- technology transfer;
- imperial/colonial inheritance;
- policy diffusion;
- historiography itself.

Those connections are often exactly what historians care about. A system should not erase them in the name of independence; it should make them visible and adjust the evidential interpretation accordingly.

This creates another contrast with ordinary analogy benchmarks: the source cases are not IID examples drawn from a neutral universe. They have histories **with each other**.

The frontier is therefore not merely multi-analogy integration, but **dependence-aware historical precedent integration**.
