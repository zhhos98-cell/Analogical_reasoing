# Representation Ladder — Historical Analogy from Time-Series Shape to Mechanism

**Snapshot: 21 August 2026.**

The historical-analogy literature is easier to understand if systems are ordered by **what a historical precedent is represented as**. Current systems occupy a ladder from very narrow statistical episodes to rich causal event structures.

The central empirical tension is:

> **As the representation becomes historically richer, the analogue becomes more meaningful but harder to calibrate, validate, and control.**

---

## Level 0 — historical observation / timestamp

A past period is an observation in the training sample. Similarity emerges through model weights or predictor-space proximity.

### Example: Dual Interpretation of Machine Learning Forecasts

The current forecast is exactly decomposed into weighted historical observations. The weights can be read as a portfolio of analogous past macroeconomic periods.

**Strength:** mathematically inspectable contribution of each past observation; straightforward out-of-sample evaluation.

**Weakness:** proximity in predictor space may have little correspondence to a historian's or causal analyst's concept of a shared mechanism.

---

## Level 1 — trajectory shape

A precedent is a historical temporal sequence whose **shape** resembles the current sequence.

### Examples

- Shape Finder / PaCE for armed conflict;
- HAL-Net for epidemic forecasting.

**Representation:** levels, rises/falls, timing, lag, volatility; usually matched through Dynamic Time Warping or related distance functions.

**Strength:** transparent analogue retrieval; historical futures provide an empirical predictive distribution; uncertainty can be estimated from analogue dispersion.

**Weakness:** two trajectories may look alike while being generated by entirely different mechanisms.

---

## Level 2 — multimodal regime / context

A precedent is a period whose joint contextual state resembles the target.

### Example: History Rhymes

Financial-news semantics are embedded jointly with macro indicators. Retrieval is restricted to earlier periods.

**Representation:** text + CPI + unemployment + yield spread + GDP growth or similar state variables.

**Strength:** richer than pure trajectory shape and useful under regime shift/OOD tests.

**Weakness:** learned proximity remains an indirect proxy for transferable mechanism.

---

## Level 3 — typed event / relational record

A precedent is an event in an explicit event schema or knowledge graph.

### Example: AnRe

Historical temporal-KG events are retrieved and replayed as analogical reasoning examples for future-event prediction.

**Representation:** subject–relation–object–time and short/long historical neighborhoods.

**Strength:** relations become explicit and machine-comparable.

**Weakness:** the ontology is supplied by the benchmark; the difficult problem of deciding what historical relation matters has largely been solved upstream.

---

## Level 4 — named historical event with multidimensional description

A precedent is a recognizable historical event represented across broad descriptive dimensions.

### Example: Past Meets Present

**Representation:** topic, background, process, result, with abstract vs literal similarity.

**Strength:** open historical event generation becomes possible; analogues can cross domain/entity boundaries.

**Weakness:** descriptive similarity still does not specify which relation causally supports a lesson.

---

## Level 5 — mechanism / structural-role event

A precedent is a causal structure whose domain-specific variables instantiate recurring roles.

### Example: CANA / ADR

**Representation:** preconditions, temporal chain, mechanism, outcome, structural positions such as trigger / enabler / amplifier / mediator / outcome.

**Strength:** rich enough to support hidden-factor inference and multiple partial precedents. CANA also records per-analogy limitations and distinguishes which precedent informs which part of the target.

**Weakness:** causal representations are contestable; evaluation is expensive; mechanism-transfer validity is difficult to estimate; the system does not yet convert each disanalogy into a claim-level executable transfer boundary.

---

# The frontier curve

| Level | Analogue representation | Historical semantic richness | OOS/calibration maturity | Transfer-control difficulty |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | weighted past observations | low | high | low–medium |
| 1 | trajectory shape | low–medium | high | medium |
| 2 | multimodal regime/context | medium | medium–high | medium |
| 3 | typed event graph | medium | medium–high | medium |
| 4 | multidimensional named event | high | medium–low | high |
| 5 | causal mechanism / structural roles | very high | early | very high |

This is not a claim that Level 5 is always better. The levels answer different questions. A trajectory analogue can outperform a richer model precisely because it refuses to infer more structure than the data support.

The research challenge is to move **calibration and transfer discipline upward** without throwing away the semantic richness that makes historical comparison useful.

---

# What would Level 6 look like?

A plausible next level is not simply a more detailed causal graph. It would be a **plural, selective, evidence-updating historical representation**:

1. maintain multiple candidate representations of the target;
2. retrieve different precedents under different representations;
3. align shared structural roles;
4. explicitly represent disanalogies;
5. issue projection-level transfer licenses/vetoes;
6. attach uncertainty to each projected claim;
7. seek evidence that discriminates competing analogies;
8. revise the event representation when a projected lesson fails;
9. retain positive and negative applicability boundaries for later cases.

This would turn historical analogy from `similarity retrieval` into a **continual model-selection and transfer-control process**.
